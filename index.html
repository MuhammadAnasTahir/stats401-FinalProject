    ContextWhy does this market matter?

    Introduction

    A visual report on where transfer money moves, who receives it, and whether it changes what happens on the pitch.

    European football's transfer market is often discussed through record fees and individual deals. This project examines its wider structure across the five major leagues: Premier League, La Liga, the Bundesliga, Serie A, and Ligue 1. It compares the distribution of recorded transfer fees, traces money between leagues and countries, tests whether pre-signing rumour coverage is associated with what a deal costs and how the player is valued afterwards, and relates spending to results on the pitch.

    Why It Matters

    A single record signing makes the headlines, but it sits inside a market that moves billions of euros between leagues every year. Fans, journalists, and analysts argue about whether big spending buys success, which leagues depend on selling players to survive, and whether the transfer rumours that fill the sports media tell us anything about what a deal will really cost. Looking at the whole market, rather than one deal at a time, lets us check those claims against ten seasons of evidence and shows the structure underneath the headlines.

    Research Questions

    The overall question is: how does money move through the European transfer market, and what factors shape the financial outcomes of transfers? It breaks into three questions that organise the rest of the page.

    1. Market structure. How have transfer spending and cross-league flows changed over time, and which clubs and leagues act as buyers, sellers, hubs, or feeders?
    2. Spending and performance. To what extent is club spending associated with later sporting performance, and which clubs over- or under-perform relative to their spending?
    3. Rumours and outcomes. Before a signing, how are rumour intensity, tone, and agent-attributed reports associated with the final fee and the change in a player's market value?

    What We Use

    Two sources answer these questions. The Transfermarkt dataset supplies about 1.1 million records of transfers, player valuations, and match results, from which we take fees, dates, buying and selling clubs, leagues, player positions, market values, and points per game for 2016/17 to 2025/26. A second dataset, 3,221 transfer-window articles from the Guardian, supplies the rumour side: each article is linked to a completed transfer and scored for intensity, tone, and explicit agent attribution.

    How Visualization Helps

    Each question needs a different view. A treemap and an annual line show how spending is concentrated and how it has grown. A chord diagram and a map show direction, with arrows from buyers to sellers between leagues and between countries. A scatterplot relates spending to the following season's results, and a box plot and a streamgraph show how rumour coverage builds and whether it lines up with the price paid. Filters, season controls, and linked selections let the viewer move between the overview and a single league, club, or position.

    Who It Is For

    The page is written for football fans, analysts, journalists, and anyone interested in the economics of sport. No background in statistics is needed. Where a chart shows an association, the text says so, and the findings section at the end separates what the data show from what they cannot show.

    How to Read This Page

    Use the navigation bar at the top of the page to jump to any section; it follows you as you scroll. The page moves from the data, to the interactions and how we evaluate them, to the visualizations in order: where the money goes, the rumour cycle, and whether spending buys results. It ends with the findings for each research question.

    ContextWhat data do we use?

    Dataset

    This section documents our two data sources, the cleaning steps applied to them, and the aggregation that turns roughly 1.1 million match, transfer, and valuation records, plus 3,221 scraped news articles, into the tables each visualization loads. Every processing step lives in the dataprocessing/ scripts in the repository, so the full pipeline is reproducible end to end.

    Raw Data

    The primary source is the CC0-licensed Transfermarkt dataset repository, downloaded as seven CSV files covering clubs, competitions, games, club-level match results, players, historical market valuations, and transfers. Together these hold roughly 1.1 million rows: every recorded transfer with its fee and clubs, every historical player valuation, every match and its club-level result, and reference data for every player, club, and competition. The raw tables are shown below.

    TableRowsWhat it holds
    transfers175,166Every recorded transfer: player, origin and destination club, date, fee
    player_valuations656,302Historical market-value snapshots per player
    club_games177,917One row per club per match: goals, result, league position
    games88,959Match metadata: competition, season, date, scoreline
    players50,150Player profiles: position, age, nationality
    clubs797Club metadata: league, squad size, stadium
    competitions66League and competition reference data

    Cleaning

    dataprocessing/clean_raw_data.py runs the same pass over every table: normalizing column names and encodings, coercing ID, numeric, and date columns to their proper types, dropping rows missing a required key (a player, a club, a date), removing duplicate keys, and discarding records dated after our July 2026 cutoff. Transfers with a negative fee or market value are treated as unknown rather than dropped. The transfers table lost rows in this pass and 175,166 records became 174,528 once records missing a season or a club on either side of the deal were removed. Every other table cleaned through with its row count intact.

    Processed Tables

    From the cleaned tables, the dataprocessing/build_*.py scripts aggregate to the club-season level, covering spending and income per club, points per game, final league position, and a squad market-value snapshot. The output is restricted to the top five leagues and the ten most recent completed seasons, from 2016/17 through 2025/26.

    Five chart-ready tables sit on top of that. treemap_data.csv has 974 club-season rows and feeds the composition view and the annual spending trend. spending_performance.csv has 754 rows, because each club's spending in one season is paired with its league results in the following season (spending seasons 2016/17 to 2024/25 against performance seasons 2017/18 to 2025/26). league_transfer_flows.csv has 3,308 rows and sums transfer fees and counts between the five leagues plus two catch-all groups, "Other Europe" and "Outside Europe / Unknown", for every buying-league to selling-league pair in each season, split by player position and fee status. club_pair_flows.csv has 20,411 rows and keeps the same flows at club level, so the chord tooltips can name the biggest individual deals; its totals match the league flows exactly. country_transfer_flows.csv has 5,617 rows and holds the same measures at country level for the map.

    Fees are summed only where recorded; a missing fee stays missing for fee-coverage counts and is never recoded as a zero fee. Transfer records do not provide a sufficiently consistent loan-status field here, so loans, returns, and permanent deals are not claimed to be separated. Country flows use club-country assignments for the transfer season, retain only pairs with a known country at both ends, and use a local Natural Earth/world-atlas country topology for the basemap.

    How the Rumour Data Was Built

    We collected 3,221 transfer-window articles from the Guardian Open Platform API, one full article body each, spread across all ten seasons. Each article is linked to a completed transfer when a distinctive player-name token and a club name both appear in the article within 120 days before the signing. We then classify tone from a keyword lexicon and flag articles that explicitly cite a player's agent. The raw corpus, one JSON file per article with its full text, is stored in data/rawdata/rumors/guardian/, and the article text is courtesy of the Guardian Open Platform, used here for a student project.

    Because this half is built from scraped text and keyword rules, it is not perfectly accurate, and we report its limits openly. 86% of articles linked to a real transfer; 4,403 transfers carry at least one article. Tone keywords match 74% of articles, so the remaining 26% fall into the neutral band by default rather than a confidently neutral reading. 91% of linked transfers have a market-value change, while only 39% have a disclosed fee, so the fee-premium view is limited to deals with a reported fee. Agent attribution is an explicit text match only, never inferred. The full accounting lives in data/processed/rumor_data_quality.csv.

    Four chart-ready tables come out of this pipeline. rumor_articles.csv has 16,720 rows, one for every article-to-transfer link, which is more than the number of articles because one article can cover several transfers. rumor_outcomes.csv has 4,403 rows, one per linked transfer, with its rumour intensity, tone mix, agent flag, fee premium, and market-value change. rumor_timeline.csv has 54 rows of weekly article counts by tone before the signing, and rumor_highlights.csv has the six most-covered transfers with a disclosed fee and a market-value change.

    MethodHow can readers explore it?

    Interaction & Animation Plan

    The spending views (treemap, league flows, map, and scatterplot) share one season, league, and optional club selection. Changing a season selector updates the others where data for that season are available, while each chart's Play control advances only its own view. The scatterplot pairs a spending season with the following season's results. Clicking a treemap league heading or a chord arc selects that league, and All leagues clears it. Clicking a bubble pins that club's season-by-season trajectory, but does not imply club-level flows in the league or country views. The position filter is shared by the league and country flow views, whereas the map's fee-status filter is local to the map. The three rumour views are standalone and do not respond to these shared selections.

    Market Composition

    • The season selector redraws the treemap with an eased 720ms transition, so club rectangles resize and reflow rather than cut instantly between seasons.
    • Play steps automatically through all ten seasons, about 1.5 seconds each, so a viewer can watch spending share shift without touching the control every time.
    • Hovering or keyboard-focusing a club cell raises a tooltip with spending, income, net spend, and transfer count, and outlines the cell. This is how a single-number rectangle becomes a comparable, inspectable data point.

    Click a league heading to focus its clubs; All leagues restores the complete composition. Hovering a point on the annual line shows that season's total spending.

    Transfer Flows

    • The season, metric, position, and fee-status controls redraw the ribbons and arcs with a 500ms eased transition.
    • Hovering a ribbon shows the buying league, the selling league, the fees and number of transfers between them, and the three biggest club-to-club deals in that flow. This lets a viewer isolate one flow inside a busy diagram and see which clubs drive it.
    • Hovering a league's outer arc shows what that league spent on incoming transfers, what it received from outgoing ones, its net spending, and its incoming and outgoing transfer counts. Clicking the arc selects that league in the other views.
    • Choosing Free transfer or Unknown fee switches the chord to transfer counts, because those deals carry no recorded fee to draw.

    Transfer fees and transfer counts are independent metric choices; unknown fees are not treated as zero-fee transfers.

    Geographic Transfer Flows

    • The Season dropdown gives each of the ten seasons its own flow map, and it moves the other season-based charts to the same season. Each curve joins a buying country to a selling country, and its width encodes the selected metric, so larger flows read as thicker lines.
    • Hovering near a curve highlights it and fades the others to a lighter tone, and a tooltip gives its fees and transfer count. A wide invisible hover zone makes thin lines easy to reach.
    • The plus, minus, and reset buttons in the corner of the map zoom in, zoom out, and return to the default Europe-centred view. Dragging pans the map, while scroll-wheel zoom is turned off so the page keeps scrolling normally.

    Metric, position, fee status, and the number of flows (top 15 or top 30) are chosen independently; free transfers are shown by count because they carry no fee.

    Transfer Rumours

    • The most-covered transfer cards show four players at first, and Show More reveals the other two. Each card traces market value, fee paid, and value afterwards.
    • Hovering a box in the box plot shows its group size, median, and quartiles, and the two panels keep separate scales for the two outcomes.
    • Hovering a band in the streamgraph highlights that tone and dims the others, and its tooltip gives the number of article mentions and the tone's share.

    The rumour views describe associations in scraped text; they do not respond to the shared season, league, or club selection.

    Spending and Performance

    • The season selector redraws the scatterplot with a 650ms eased transition, so a club's bubble visibly travels between its old and new (spending, points-per-game) position. This is what makes a change in strategy legible from one season to the next.
    • Play animates through all ten seasons automatically, the same pacing as the treemap, and Clear club removes a pinned club.
    • Hovering a bubble shows the club, league, spending season, following performance season, spending, points per game, expected points per game, and squad value.
    • A fitted log-spending trend line gives the expected points per game for the selected sample; a bubble above the line scored more points than its spending predicts and a bubble below it scored fewer, and the tooltip gives each club's difference from the trend.

    A club click traces available seasons only. The fitted line describes an association, not a causal effect of spending.

    MethodHow do we test the design?

    Evaluation Plan

    We will evaluate each of the eight views against the task it is designed for, and we will check that the controls are usable without us standing over someone's shoulder to explain them.

    What We Evaluate

    We will evaluate whether each view supports its intended task: composition and comparison in the treemap, trend reading in the line chart, flow direction and magnitude in the chord diagram and the map, comparison of distributions in the box plot, change over time in the streamgraph, and relationship and outlier detection in the scatterplot. We will also check that the season controls, filters, zoom buttons, tooltips, and play animation are discoverable without instructions, and that the rumour views are not misread as showing cause and effect.

    How We Evaluate It

    We will run short think-aloud sessions with four to six classmates or football club members outside the group. Each participant will get concrete tasks such as "which club spent the most in 2023/24," "which league is the biggest net buyer," "which country receives the most money from England," "do heavily rumoured deals cost more," and "find a club that over-performed its spending," and we will watch where they hesitate, misread a label, or miss a control.

    What We Collect

    We will collect task completion and time-to-answer, notes on specific points of confusion, and a short post-task questionnaire on label clarity and trust in the numbers. We will also fold in the TA and peer feedback received at the interim check-in, and will use all of it in the final refinement of the page.

    ConclusionWhat the data show

    Findings

    Each group of findings answers one of the three research questions. The headline result is that transfer spending is clearly followed by better results on the pitch.

    Market Structure

    • Spending has grown, and England takes the largest share. Recorded transfer-fee spending by clubs in the five leagues rose from €2.93B in 2016/17 to €7.72B in 2025/26, about 2.6 times as much, with a dip to €3.47B in 2020/21. The Premier League's share ranged from 28.6% (2019/20) to 53.6% (2022/23) and was 51.8% in 2025/26. Over the ten seasons Chelsea spent the most (€2.64B), ahead of Manchester City (€2.03B) and Manchester United (€1.86B).
    • The Premier League is the market's net buyer. Premier League clubs bought €22.1B of players and sold €10.4B over the ten seasons, a net of +€11.6B. Serie A (+€1.4B), La Liga (+€0.5B), and the Bundesliga (+€0.3B) are close to balanced, and Ligue 1 is the only one of the five that sells more than it buys (−€0.6B).
    • English clubs pull money from every other big league. The four largest cross-border corridors are English clubs buying from German (€2.70B), French (€2.51B), Italian (€2.06B), and Spanish (€1.98B) clubs. About a third (33%) of paid-fee money stays inside a single country.

    Spending and Performance

    • Clubs that spend more finish higher the next season. In every one of the nine spending seasons, the top quarter of spenders among the five leagues' clubs earned more points per game the following season than the bottom quarter. On average the top spenders collected 1.78 points per game against 1.17 for the lowest spenders, a gap of about 0.6 points per game, or roughly 23 points over a 38-match season. The typical top-quarter spender finished 5th in its league, while the typical bottom-quarter spender finished 13th.
    • The link is consistent across seasons. The correlation between a club's log spending and its next-season points per game is positive in all nine seasons, from 0.35 to 0.67 and about 0.51 on average. Half of the top-quarter spenders finished in the top four of their league, more than twice the share a randomly chosen club would have.
    • Some clubs beat their budget and some waste it. Bubbles above the dashed line scored more points than their spending predicts and bubbles below it scored fewer, and the scatterplot lets a viewer pin a club to follow its path across seasons.

    Rumours and Outcomes

    • Rumour coverage builds sharply toward the signing. Half of all linked article mentions (50%) fall in the last two weeks before a signing, and 73% in the last four. Optimistic reporting grows as the deal nears, from 30% of mentions four to five weeks out to 42% in the signing week, while doubtful reporting remains throughout.
    • Heavily rumoured deals cost somewhat more over market value. The median fee-to-value ratio is 1.18× for low-intensity transfers, 1.17× for medium, and 1.23× for high, and the boxes overlap heavily.
    • Rumour intensity does not change how a player's value moves afterwards. Among players whose valuation was updated, the median change is +11.1% for both low and high intensity and +7.9% for medium, while the average change is smaller for high-intensity transfers (+35%) than for low-intensity ones (+54%). One possible reading is that the most-covered players are already valued near their peak, so coverage follows value rather than creating it.

    Limitations

    • The rumour data come from one outlet, the Guardian, and tone keywords match only 74% of mentions; the rest default to neutral.
    • Only 39% of linked transfers have a disclosed fee, so the fee-premium comparison covers fewer deals than the market-value one.
    • Transfermarkt updates valuations only a few times a year, so about one in four transfers show no value change and are left out of the market-value panel.
    • Loans, returns, and permanent deals are not separated, and valuable players attract more coverage whatever the rumours say, so none of these results should be read as cause and effect.

    Conclusion

    Higher spending is followed by better results. Across ten seasons the transfer market grew about 2.6 times, with English clubs as its largest buyers and most other leagues close to balanced, and the clubs that spent the most scored about 0.6 more points per game the next season than the clubs that spent the least, in every season we measured. Rumour coverage shows how close a deal is, building sharply in the final two weeks, but it does not clearly predict what a player costs or how a player's value changes. The main message is that money moves a club up the table, and that England's spending power shapes the market's flows.
