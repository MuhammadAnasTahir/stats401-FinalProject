The Football Transfer Market

Ten seasons of transfer fees, league-to-league flows, and club performance, traced through the Premier League, La Liga, the Bundesliga, Serie A, and Ligue 1.

By Zihan (Martina) Ma & Muhammad Anas Tahir

STATS 401 Final Project

A visual report on where transfer money moves, who receives it, and whether it changes what happens on the pitch.

European football's transfer market is often discussed through record fees and individual deals. This project examines its wider structure across the five major leagues: Premier League, La Liga, the Bundesliga, Serie A, and Ligue 1. It compares the distribution of recorded transfer fees, traces money between leagues, and relates spending to results on the pitch.

Dataset

This section documents our raw data, the cleaning steps applied to it, and the aggregation that turns roughly 1.1 million match, transfer, and valuation records into the season-level tables each visualization loads. Every processing step lives in the dataprocessing/ scripts in the repository, so the full pipeline is reproducible end to end.

Raw Data

The primary source is the CC0-licensed Transfermarkt dataset repository, downloaded as seven CSV files covering clubs, competitions, games, club-level match results, players, historical market valuations, and transfers. Together these hold roughly 1.1 million rows: every recorded transfer with its fee and clubs, every historical player valuation, every match and its club-level result, and reference data for every player, club, and competition. The raw tables are shown below.

Cleaning

dataprocessing/clean_raw_data.py runs the same pass over every table: normalizing column names and encodings, coercing ID, numeric, and date columns to their proper types, dropping rows missing a required key (a player, a club, a date), removing duplicate keys, and discarding records dated after our July 2026 cutoff. Transfers with a negative fee or market value are treated as unknown rather than dropped. The transfers table lost rows in this pass and 175,166 records became 174,528 once records missing a season or a club on either side of the deal were removed. Every other table cleaned through with its row count intact.

Processed Tables

From the cleaned tables, the dataprocessing/build_*.py scripts aggregate to the club-season level, covering spending and income per club, points per game, final league position, and a squad market-value snapshot. The output is restricted to the top five leagues and the ten most recent completed seasons, from 2016/17 through 2025/26.

Four chart-ready tables sit on top of that. Both treemap_data.csv and spending_performance.csv contain 974 club-season rows and feed the composition and performance views. The third, league_transfer_flows.csv, has 449 rows and sums transfer fees and counts between the five leagues plus two catch-all groups, "Other Europe" and "Outside Europe / Unknown", for every buying-league to selling-league pair in each season.

Fees are summed only where recorded; a missing fee stays missing for fee-coverage counts and is never recoded as a zero fee. Transfer records do not provide a sufficiently consistent loan-status field here, so loans, returns, and permanent deals are not claimed to be separated. Country flows use club-country assignments for the transfer season, retain only pairs with a known country at both ends, and use a local Natural Earth/world-atlas country topology for the basemap.

Interaction & Animation Plan

The views share one season, league, and optional club selection. Changing any season selector updates the others where data for that season are available; each chart's Play control advances only its own view. The performance view pairs a spending season with the following season's results. Clicking a treemap league or a chord arc selects that league; All leagues clears it. Clicking a bubble pins the club's available season trajectory, but does not imply club-level flows in the league or country views. Position filters are shared by the two flow views. The map's fee-status filter is local to the map, so its geographic framing can respond to the selected type of transfer.

Market Composition

  • The season selector redraws the treemap with an eased 720ms transition, so club rectangles resize and reflow rather than cut instantly between seasons.
  • Play steps automatically through all ten seasons, about 1.5 seconds each, so a viewer can watch spending share shift without touching the control every time.
  • Hovering or keyboard-focusing a club cell raises a tooltip with spending, income, net spend, and transfer count, and outlines the cell. This is how a single-number rectangle becomes a comparable, inspectable data point.

Click a league heading to focus its clubs; All leagues restores the complete composition.

Transfer Flows

  • The season selector swaps the fee matrix and redraws both ribbons and arcs with a 500ms eased transition.
  • Hovering a ribbon brings it to full opacity against the others and shows the buying league, selling league, and total fee. This lets a viewer isolate one flow inside a busy diagram.
  • Hovering a league's outer arc shows that league's total recorded volume across every flow touching it.

Transfer fees and transfer counts are independent metric choices; unknown fees are not treated as zero-fee transfers.

Spending and Performance

  • The season selector redraws the scatterplot with a 650ms eased transition, so a club's bubble visibly travels between its old and new (spending, points-per-game) position. This is what makes a change in strategy legible from one season to the next.
  • Play animates through all ten seasons automatically, the same pacing as the treemap.
  • Hovering a bubble shows the club, league, spending season, following performance season, spending, points per game, squad value, and whether the club over- or under-performed its spending.
  • A fitted log-spending trend line gives the expected points per game for the selected sample; bubble outlines show whether a club is above or below that trend.

A club click traces available seasons only. The fitted line describes an association, not a causal effect of spending.

Visualizations

Four working visualizations, built from the processed data above. The visualizations tell the concentration of the spending, transfer fee movement between the leagues, and whether spending is related to performance.

Market Composition

Each rectangle is a club, sized by its recorded transfer-fee spending that season and grouped by league. Bigger rectangles mean larger recorded outlays. Step through seasons or hit Play to see which leagues and clubs pull further ahead of the pack over time. Area encodes recorded transfer-fee spending, color encodes net spending, and enclosing groups identify leagues. Net spending means spending minus income. In 2025/26, the Premier League accounts for 51.8% of displayed top-five spending; the largest club accounts for 6.2%.

Transfer Flows

Transfer fees connect Europe’s top leagues with each other and with clubs across the rest of the world. Arrows run from buying league to selling league; each outer arc is the total of flows entering and leaving that group. In 2025/26, the Premier League received €2.34B as a selling league; the metric control also exposes transfer counts.

Spending and Performance

Each point pairs a club's recorded transfer-fee spending in one season with its points per game in the following season. The chart reports the current filtered sample's correlation and relative performance gap; these are associations, not causal effects. Performance here means following-season domestic-league points per game, not cup or European performance.

Geographic Transfer Flows

Country flows are built before league grouping: season-specific club matches supply country first, then the club reference table fills missing country assignments. This view includes only flows involving at least one top-five-league club. Lines point from buyer country to seller country; only the top 30 non-domestic flows are drawn, while domestic transfers are summarized separately. Transfers with no country in either source are excluded rather than placed arbitrarily.

Transfer Rumours

Beyond the completed deals, this half of the project asks whether the pre-signing rumour cycle relates to the final financial outcome of a transfer. We built a second, scraped dataset of transfer-rumour articles, linked each article to a completed transfer, and derived rumour intensity, tone, and explicit agent attribution for every linked deal.

How the Rumour Data Was Built

We collected 3,221 transfer-window articles from the Guardian Open Platform API, one full article body each, spread across all ten seasons. Each article is linked to a completed transfer when a distinctive player-name token and a club name both appear in the article within 120 days before the signing. We then classify tone from a keyword lexicon and flag articles that explicitly cite a player's agent.

Because this half is built from scraped text and keyword rules, it is not perfectly accurate, and we report its limits openly. 86% of articles linked to a real transfer; 4,403 transfers carry at least one article. Tone keywords match 74% of articles, so the remaining 26% fall into a combined "neutral / unclear" band rather than a confident neutral. 91% of linked transfers have a market-value change, while only 39% have a disclosed fee, so the fee-premium view is limited to deals with a reported fee. Agent attribution is an explicit text match only, never inferred. The full accounting lives in data/processed/rumor_data_quality.csv.

Most-Covered Transfers

The six transfers that drew the most rumour coverage in our corpus, ranked purely by linked-article count among deals with a disclosed fee and a market-value change. Each card traces the player's market value into the fee actually paid and the valuation that followed.

Rumour Intensity and Transfer Outcomes

Transfers are split into low, medium, and high rumour intensity by their linked-article count. The two panels show both financial outcomes side by side: on the left, the fee premium (fee paid relative to market value); on the right, the change in the player's market value around the transfer. Each panel keeps its own scale because the two outcomes are measured in different units.

Rumour Volume and Tone Before a Signing

Linked articles are aligned by how many weeks before the signing they appeared, then stacked by tone. The shape shows how coverage builds toward deadline and how the mix of optimistic, doubtful, and neutral reporting shifts as a deal nears completion.

Evaluation Plan

For the four implemented non-rumor views, we will check that each one actually supports the task it is designed for, and that the controls are usable without us standing over someone's shoulder to explain them.

What We Will Evaluate

We will evaluate whether each view supports its intended task: composition and comparison in the treemap, flow direction and magnitude in the chord diagram, and relationship and outlier detection in the scatterplot. We also want to make sure that the season controls, tooltips, and play animation are discoverable without instructions.

How We Will Evaluate It

We will organise short think-aloud sessions with four to six classmates or football club members outside the group. We will give them concrete tasks such as "which club spent the most in 2023/24," "which league is the biggest net buyer," and "find a club that over-performed its spending," and watch where they hesitate, misread a label, or miss a control.

What We Will Collect

We want to collect task completion and time-to-answer, notes on specific points of confusion, and a short post-task questionnaire on label clarity and trust in the numbers. We will also fold in TA and peer feedback from the interim check-in walk-around, and use all of it in the Week 6 to 7 refinement phase.
